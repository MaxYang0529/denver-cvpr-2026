# ALTITUDE SURVEY
### A Visual Philosophy

---

**Altitude Survey** treats a journey as fieldwork. It borrows the disciplined eye of the topographic surveyor and the patient annotator — someone who climbs, observes, measures, and records — and turns the act of travel into a meticulously documented expedition. Every place becomes a *station*; every photograph becomes a *plate*; every day becomes a *transect* drawn across rising and falling ground. The aesthetic is that of a master's field journal: cream survey paper, fine ink contour lines, hairline registration marks, and the quiet authority of coordinates set in monospace. It must read as though a single devoted hand labored over it for weeks, aligning every mark with painstaking attention.

The philosophy lives in **space and form** through the contour line. Faint topographic curves thread beneath the composition, never decorative noise but a structural undercurrent — the land breathing under the page. Generous margins frame each plate like a specimen pinned for study. Form is rectilinear and exact, broken only by the organic wander of the contours, so that precision and wilderness hold each other in tension. Nothing crowds; everything is given room to be observed.

In **color and material**, the palette is restrained to the high country at altitude: warm survey-paper cream, deep spruce ink, cold slate, and a single signal accent of sunset rust — the colour of alpenglow on rock and of a surveyor's flag. Photographs are the only saturated objects on the page; the surrounding system stays muted so that each image arrives like a window cut into paper. This chromatic discipline is the product of deep expertise — every tone calibrated, nothing arbitrary.

**Scale and rhythm** descend from the survey ledger. Enormous thin display serifs name the destinations; beside them, tiny monospace annotations carry the dry data — latitude, longitude, elevation, station index. The dramatic difference in scale *is* the hierarchy: the eye reads the headline as terrain and the labels as instrument readings. Repetition governs rhythm — each day a repeating module, each plate a repeating frame — so the work rewards sustained viewing, the way a careful map reveals more the longer it is read.

**Composition and balance** obey an invisible grid, the way triangulated survey points anchor a map. Registration crosshairs sit in corners; hairline rules separate strata of information; elevation profiles run as quiet baselines. Text is sparse and essential — a place name, a coordinate, a single line of observation — never an explanatory paragraph competing with the image. Information lives in the arrangement, in the relationship of plate to label to line, not in prose.

Above all, **Altitude Survey** must feel hand-built and exact, the master-level execution of someone at the top of their craft. It is the paradox of using cold analytical instruments — grids, coordinates, contours, registration marks — to hold something warm and fleeting: the memory of a journey through thin bright air. The result should look like an artifact that proves a trip can be studied, mapped, and understood through careful attention — meticulously crafted, the product of countless patient hours.

*Expressed as: a single-page field-journal website. Subject woven invisibly into the form — a journey surveyed at altitude, recorded with the rigor reserved for observable phenomena.*
